# 10 — Game AI

## A) Decision making

1. **Finite state machine (FSM):** states (Idle, Patrol, Chase, Attack, Flee)
   with transitions. Simple and enough for most small games; gets messy past
   ~10 states.
2. **Behavior trees:** a tree of selectors ("try these until one works"),
   sequences ("do these in order") and conditions. Standard in Unreal and most
   AAA games since the mid-2000s (Halo 2 popularized them). Easy to extend.
3. **Utility AI:** score every possible action by how useful it is right now
   (hunger, danger, distance) and pick the best. The Sims is the famous
   example. Good for lifelike NPCs.
4. **GOAP (goal-oriented action planning):** the AI plans a sequence of actions
   to reach a goal (F.E.A.R.'s soldiers flanking and taking cover). Emergent
   but expensive and harder to control.

## B) Movement and navigation

1. **Navigation mesh (navmesh):** walkable areas stored as polygons; paths are
   found across polygons with **A*** search, then smoothed (string pulling).
   Libraries: recast-navigation (has WebAssembly builds for the web).
2. **Steering behaviors** (Craig Reynolds): seek, flee, arrive (slow near the
   target), wander, avoid obstacles, separation/alignment/cohesion for crowds
   and flocks.
3. **Splines** for fixed routes: racing lines, traffic lanes, patrols.

## C) Sports and racing AI

1. Sports AI uses **formations and roles** (positions on the field) plus
   decision rules (pass, shoot, defend) with skill-based error.
2. Opponents in golf/bowling are usually simulated shots with random error
   scaled by difficulty.
3. Racing AI: see `07-racing.md`.

## D) Making AI fun, not smart

1. **Telegraph** intentions (an enemy winds up before attacking).
2. **Add reaction delays and aim error**; perfect AI feels unfair.
3. **Difficulty** = reaction time, accuracy, aggression, not extra health only.
4. **Readable states:** players should see when an NPC notices them
   (animation, sound, icon).
