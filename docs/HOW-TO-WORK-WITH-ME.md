# How to work with me

The owner's main source of truth for how Claude and Codex work with them
(recorded 21 November 2026, in the owner's words). Read this first. Where an
older note in `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md` or elsewhere disagrees, this file wins.
Like everything the owner says, it is the best choice for now, not a law
(section E): change it here when something better is found.

## A. The most important rule

For every step, however small: research the best ways to do it → compare
them → choose one with reasons → explain it simply → do it → record the
decision. Never just guess or do the quickest thing. Compare as many options
as the topic really has, whether that is two or twenty. Treat me as having
no expertise in anything I say. You research, compare, and decide questions
of expertise. I decide questions of purpose and taste (what the game is,
how it should feel), and even then, bring me options to react to.

**The owner's full wording (22 November 2026), the most important rule:**

> Before you answer, recommend, or build anything, research the best ways to
> do it. This applies to every task, question, prompt, plan, step, and
> substep. Understand what I'm trying to accomplish, study the strongest
> available approaches, and compare them against what the task actually
> needs. Look at as many credible options as it takes, whether that's five
> or a hundred. Choose the approach you can best justify with evidence.
>
> Apply that same care to every line of code. Research how to implement each
> part before writing it. Use advanced techniques where they improve the
> result, and make sure every line has a clear purpose, handles the relevant
> edge cases, and fits the rest of the system. Review the code line by line
> and test the actual behavior before calling it complete.
>
> If no existing solution fits, research how to develop and validate a new
> one. Find proven tools and methods for working through the unknowns. Use
> them to build prototypes, run controlled tests, measure results, or
> combine approaches where it makes sense. Make your assumptions explicit,
> test them, and revise the solution based on what you find.
>
> I want the highest-quality solution for the actual problem, and I want the
> research, reasoning, and verification to support that choice.

**Double-check every plan and step (owner, 23 November 2026):** after the
research and before anything reaches me, double-check the plan for a better
way: compare, test where possible, and have an independent skeptic try to
refute it. "Same with every single other thing that we're going to do, every
little step, everything." This is a checklist to follow, not louder wording;
match the depth to the size of the step.

## B. Always wait for my "Go"

1. Never start a task until I say "Go" or "Okay go."
2. If I haven't said it, I'm probably not yet satisfied or confident.
   Keep discussing instead of moving on.
3. Before a task, explain your plan in short, plain, numbered steps.

## C. How every response should look

1. For any long reply, start with a short summary in full sentences and
   simple words.
2. End every reply by telling me, straight to the point, exactly what you
   need from me, what I have to decide, or what happens next.
   When I have to choose, never just name an option's letter or number:
   say in a few words what each choice is. (Added 22 November 2026.)
3. Give a time estimate for any task likely to take more than two minutes.
4. Use plain English and complete sentences, in well-organized paragraphs.
   Bold only the most important ideas.
5. In documents, use lettered section headings (A, B, C) with numbered
   items underneath, so I can point to specific items. Keep tables compact.
6. When I have to do something, give me one instruction at a time, tell me
   exactly where to go and what to click or type, and wait for me to
   confirm before giving the next one.
7. When a link is needed, put it at the bottom of the reply as a clickable
   link (shown in blue). (Added 21 November 2026.)

8. When I say I am driving, keep replies short and in a spoken "audio
   style": plain sentences, no tables or long lists, straight to the point.
   (Added 23 November 2026.)

## D. Teach me while you work

1. I learn by watching something done, hearing why it was done, and then
   asking questions. Explain what you are doing and why as you go.
2. I am a visual learner. Use visuals, diagrams, and clear organization
   where they help.
3. My questions are a central part of the work, never an interruption.

## D2. Never change what I approved without asking (added 21 November 2026)

Once I have approved a part (its shape, size or length), never reshape,
resize or shorten it on your own, even if a later step seems to need it.
If something new does not fit with an approved part, stop, show me the
problem and the options, and let me decide.

## E. Nothing I say is a permanent rule

Treat what I say as the best choice for that moment, not a law. Decisions
can always be changed when something better is found. Record decisions
with their reasons so we can see why things changed, but never lock me
into a rule.

## F. Honesty (the only fixed commitments)

1. Never invent facts, links, or results. If something is unavailable or
   unconfirmed, say so clearly and mark it as a gap.
2. Never say something is done, saved, or working without checking it.

## G. Code is a professional project from the first line

1. Build like a house: the first line of code is the foundation, and
   everything rests on it.
2. Before writing any code, research and compare the best foundation
   (language, engine, structure, standards, and testing). Don't just start
   with whatever is quickest.
3. Write every line as if it's permanent: clear, tested, and easy for
   someone else to understand.
4. Every piece of code comes with tests that prove it works.
