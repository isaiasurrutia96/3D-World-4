# Invent Movement From Small, Measured Experiments

When nothing exists yet for what you want to build, the people who succeed do not design the whole machine at once and hope it works. They split the goal into separate jobs, build a cheap test setup for each job, try several versions side by side, measure what happens, and only then join the winning pieces together. The Wright brothers invented controlled flight this way, and the game developers who invented physics-driven characters (NaturalMotion's Euphoria, Gang Beasts, Human: Fall Flat, Totally Accurate Battle Simulator, Overgrowth and the SIMBICON research walker) followed the same loop: start with the crudest body that can move, add one or two simple "muscle" or balance rules, play with it, keep what feels right, and keep tuning for years. The best measured evidence behind this loop is that **building several prototypes in parallel beats polishing one at a time**, and that **treating each idea as a hypothesis to test leads to fewer, better changes of direction**. For this game's 1.93 m mannequin, the research points to a three-layer design: a planning layer (grown from the balance, reaching and pose work already in `motion-core`, plus the foot planner kept in its history) decides where each piece should be; joint motors in Rapier (springs built into each joint) pull the pieces toward those targets with limited strength; and the physics engine produces the actual motion, so heavy arms carry their own momentum and follow through like a ragdoll. Natural variation should come from motor-control science: **vary the details that do not change the result, and control only the ones that do**. The biggest unknowns are technical and must be measured, not assumed: Rapier's browser version gained motors on ball joints only in August 2026, still cannot limit how far a ball joint bends, and nobody has published an active ragdoll built on it or any phone timings for it. A word on the evidence: most findings come from search-result summaries, because the research session could not open most full web pages; the Rapier facts are the exception, checked against Rapier's own source files and its published package.

## A. Successful inventors tested one job at a time

Engineers, scientists and designers have documented about ten methods for inventing something new. They are less like competitors and more like tools with different jobs: some help you **frame** the problem, some **supply ideas**, some **organize the work**, and some **decide which ideas survive**. The closest historical match for inventing a movement system is the Wright brothers, because an aircraft, like an athletic body, is a moving system that must be actively controlled every moment.

### A1. The Wright brothers searched job by job, not design by design

In 1899 the Wrights concluded that "the final problem to be solved was control," while their rivals concentrated on engines or on aircraft that would be stable by themselves ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/roadtofirstflight.htm)). Their 1900 and 1901 gliders produced only **about one-third of the lift** that the published tables predicted. Instead of building another glider, they mounted test wings on a bicycle wheel to confirm the error, then built a small wind tunnel and tested **100 to 200 model wings** between September and December 1901 ([NASA Glenn](https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/K-12/airplane/wrights/results.html); [Centennial of Flight](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080917114018/http://centennialofflight.gov/essay/Wright_Bros/1901/WR3.htm)). Their 1902 glider, designed from that data, made **more than 300 flights** and was the first aircraft actively controlled on all three axes ([NASA Glenn](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/unpowered-aircraft)).

The cognitive scientist Gary Bradshaw explains why this worked. The rivals used a "design and test" strategy: build a whole aircraft, try it, and see what happens. A test of the whole machine only tells you how well it did overall, not which part to change ([Bradshaw](https://invention.psychology.msstate.edu/Tale_of_Airplane/Design.html)). Searching one function at a time (lift, then control, then power) is far more efficient ([Cognitive Science Society](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/32905/galley/23965/download)). The lesson transfers directly. A full playtest of the mannequin can tell you "the throw feels floaty," but not whether the cause is arm weight, motor strength, timing or balance. **A separate test page for each job, with live numbers on screen, is this project's wind tunnel.** A second lesson: the Wrights were misled by trusted published tables that did not fit their wing shape, and they caught the error only by measuring for themselves (sources disagree on exactly why the tables failed) ([NASA Glenn](https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/K-12/airplane/wrights/results.html)). Settings copied from another engine or a tutorial deserve the same suspicion.

### A2. Ten methods fill four different roles

**First-principles reasoning** means breaking a problem down to its basic facts and rebuilding from there instead of copying the usual answer. Its best-known example (pricing a battery's raw materials instead of accepting the industry price) is an anecdote, and no controlled study has tested the method ([Quartz](https://qz.com/972118/simple-math-is-why-elon-musks-companies-keep-doing-what-others-dont-even-consider-possible/)). **TRIZ**, developed by Genrich Altshuller from patterns in patents, states a problem as a contradiction ("improving X makes Y worse") and points to **40 inventive principles** that resolved similar contradictions before ([WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/export/sites/www/tisc/en/docs/tisc-toolkit-triz-description.pdf)). "More responsive makes it less physical" and "more stable makes it less agile" are exactly this kind of contradiction. **Morphological analysis**, Fritz Zwicky's "Zwicky box," lists each independent choice in a problem, lists the options for each choice, and combines one option per choice while crossing out combinations that clash ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_analysis_(problem-solving))).

**Analogy and biomimicry** borrow a working mechanism from another field or from nature. The nose of Japan's 500-series bullet train came from an engineer and birdwatcher who compared a train entering a tunnel to a kingfisher diving into water, but the final shape was chosen by **systematic scale-model testing**, not by the analogy itself ([Trellis](https://trellis.net/article/how-one-engineers-birdwatching-made-japans-bullet-train-better)).

**Process frameworks** organize the work. The UK Design Council's **Double Diamond** widens and narrows twice: discover, define, develop, deliver ([Design Council](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/our-resources/framework-for-innovation)). Toyota's **set-based concurrent engineering** keeps several options alive for each part and narrows them gradually by finding where every team's options overlap, instead of repeatedly reworking one chosen design ([Toche et al. 2020](https://publications.polymtl.ca/9280/1/2020_Toche_Set-based_design_review_new_directions.pdf)). **Research through design** treats the thing you build as the research itself ([Zimmerman et al.](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i262/s13/readings_pdf/Zimmerman_Research_Through_Design_as_a_Method_for_IaD_in_HCI_0.pdf)), and Donald Schön described designing as a conversation in which each attempt "talks back" and surprises reshape the plan: "it is rare that the designer has the design all in her head in advance" ([Schön interview](https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/bds/9-schon.html)).

**Learning loops** decide what survives: the scientific method (state a hypothesis, then test it), the startup "build, measure, learn" loop, the Wrights' measure-each-part approach, and Edison's organized laboratory, where up to about sixty staff screened materials systematically rather than at random ([Edison Papers](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/biographical-essays/lighting/from-research-to-development)).

| Method | Use it when | Strength of evidence | Main trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| First principles | Questioning inherited assumptions | Anecdotes only | Misses how parts interact |
| TRIZ contradictions | A trade-off blocks progress | Small student experiments | Complex; no rule for which tool to use |
| Morphological chart | Many independent choices | Practitioner reports | Too many combinations without screening |
| Analogy or biomimicry | You need a new mechanism | Student experiments, case histories | Copying the look, not the mechanism |
| Double Diamond | The problem itself is unclear | Case studies, surveys | Becomes a ritual of steps |
| Set-based (Toyota) | Parts must fit together; late changes are costly | Toyota case history | Needs discipline to keep options open |
| Wright approach | A moving, actively controlled system | Case history | Needs a test rig for each job |
| Hypothesis loop | Deciding what to keep | Randomized trials with startups | Slow if each test is expensive |

The "use it when" and "main trap" cells are judgments drawn from the sources in this section ([Ilevbare et al.](https://typeset.io/papers/a-review-of-triz-and-its-benefits-and-challenges-in-practice-24hqatxxin); [Robbins & Fu](https://doras.dcu.ie/26643/); [Bradshaw](https://invention.psychology.msstate.edu/Tale_of_Airplane/Design.html)).

### A3. Measured studies reward parallel prototypes, few unusual examples and explicit hypotheses

No study compares whole methods head to head. What has been measured are the habits they share. In Dow and colleagues' 2010 study, people designing web adverts either made several prototypes at once and got feedback on the whole set (**parallel**) or got feedback after each one (**serial**). The parallel group won on every measure, including real click rates in a live campaign of about **one million ad views**; its designs were more varied, and its members gained confidence while the serial group did not ([Dow, CMU PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~spdow/files/PrototypingAll-theLink12.pdf); [UCSD ProtoLab](https://protolab.ucsd.edu/?p=3561)). A 2022 physical design competition found the same advantage ([Murphy et al.](https://edrl.engr.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/142/2022/02/Murphy2022_Article_ComparingParallelAndIterativeP.pdf)), although these are small, mostly student samples.

Examples cut both ways. Designers shown one example solution copy its features even when told not to, and professionals do it too ([Jansson & Smith, via Crilly](https://core.ac.uk/download/237398528.pdf)). A **meta-analysis (a study that pools many studies) of 43 experiments** found that examples still help, and help most when they are **few and uncommon**; a single unusual example worked best ([Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100081707150_0x000001)). Distant, less common examples also produced more original ideas among engineering students ([Chan et al. 2011](https://edrl.engr.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/142/2022/02/2011-jmd-distancecommonness.pdf)). For the mannequin, this argues against studying a pile of similar ragdoll games and for one deliberate outside reference, such as slow-motion footage of a real athlete throwing.

The strongest evidence for the hypothesis habit comes from **randomized trials**, experiments in which chance decides who gets the method. Founders trained to treat their business idea as a theory to test made sharper decisions in a trial of **116 startups** ([Camuffo et al.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/suppl/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249)). A replication across **759 firms** found they made **one or two major changes of direction**, were less likely either never to change or to change endlessly, and that fewer changes went with better results ([CEPR](https://cepr.org/publications/dp15972); [INSEAD](https://knowledge.insead.edu/entrepreneurship/science-successful-start-ups)). TRIZ raised the originality and variety of student ideas but slightly lowered their number ([Vargas Hernandez et al.](https://typeset.io/pdf/systematic-ideation-effectiveness-study-of-triz-2pddgdhms5.pdf)), while design thinking still lacks systematic proof that it delivers value ([Robbins & Fu](https://doras.dcu.ie/26643/)). The famous marshmallow challenge, in which kindergarteners beat business graduates by building early and rebuilding often, points the same way but is workshop folklore, not peer-reviewed data ([Tom Wujec](https://www.tomwujec.com/marshmallow-challenge)).

### A4. Every method shares one core loop

Put together, the sources describe one loop. **Frame** the goal as jobs with measurable tests, and name the hardest unsolved job. **Break it down**, and write the key trade-offs as contradictions. **Widen** to several options per job. **Probe** them cheaply and in parallel. **Measure** each job on its own, then add a human judgment for what numbers miss. **Reflect**, because experienced designers develop the problem and the solution together, and surprises in one reshape the other ([Dorst & Cross](https://oro.open.ac.uk/3278)). **Narrow** deliberately, with only a few big changes of direction. Then **combine and repeat** at higher quality, re-testing earlier jobs each time. No single source publishes this loop as a standard; it is a synthesis. Its power comes from three habits more than from any named method: **parallelism** (several options at once), **isolation** (one job per test) and **explicit hypotheses** (knowing in advance which result would change the plan).

## B. Game developers found new movement by playing with crude prototypes

### B1. Movement was built first, in an empty space

Super Mario 64 began as a room of "simple lego-like blocks" where Mario could run, climb slopes and jump, built before any levels existed ([Shmuplations, Miyamoto interview](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/)). Insomniac's web-swinging in Marvel's Spider-Man was "literally the first thing we actually worked on," physics-based from the start, then tuned over "a multi-year process of iteration" that added invisible helpers, such as a push away from buildings and web attachment that reads the player's momentum ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/marvels-spider-man-how-insomniac-spun-its-web/1100-6464062/); [Cheat Code Central](https://www.cheatcc.com/articles/how-insomniac-tuned-spider-man-s-swing-to-feel-so-fast/)). Celeste's movement came from a four-day game jam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celeste_(video_game))) and was later wrapped in hidden "forgiveness" rules such as coyote time, where a jump still works for a moment after running off a ledge ([Maddy Thorson](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)). Super Mario Odyssey's capture idea came from three groups prototyping in parallel and merging their ideas ([Super Mario Wiki, summarizing a 4Gamer interview](https://www.mariowiki.com/User:Shokora/Odyssey_development)). Nintendo tested Breath of the Wild's physics in a simple top-down prototype that ran the real physics engine underneath, so its lessons carried straight into the final game ([Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/2017/03/09/zelda-team-discusses-breath-of-the-wilds-development-and-2d-zelda-prototype-at-gdc/)). Journey's team left out a jump button on purpose, because it would have turned walking into hopping ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/the-inspiration-and-goal-behind-thatgamecompanys-journey/)); a prototype is also how you discover what to remove.

The smallest unit of this work is the **spike**, a term from the Extreme Programming method: "a very simple program to explore potential solutions" that "only addresses the problem under examination" and is usually thrown away ([C2 wiki](https://c2.com/xp/SpikeSolution.html)). At a larger scale, Carnegie Mellon students made about **50 games in one semester** under a one-week limit per game, and one of them, Tower of Goo, grew into the hit World of Goo ([Gabler et al.](https://www.cs.hmc.edu/~markk/SWE_copies/gabler_prototyping.html)).

### B2. Feel becomes tunable when it is described in feel-words, sliders and graphs

Kyle Pittman's GDC talk on jumping replaced guesswork about raw physics numbers with designer words: choose how high the jump goes and how long it takes to reach the top, and let the code work out gravity and launch speed ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023148/contactUs)). One developer put the reason plainly: "Tweaking one number until something feels good is okay, but tweaking two numbers at a time is a pain" ([Archetype devlog](https://serfofcinder.itch.io/archetype-portal-to-hell/devlog/714077/character-controller-tech-talk)). The MotoGP team built live "tweakables" so they could "experiment with different numbers and immediately see the results" ([Shawn Hargreaves](https://www.shawnhargreaves.com/blog/motogp-tweakables.html)), and Ryan Darcey built frame-by-frame graphs of 3D character movement to check feel against numbers ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/analyzing-core-character-movement-in-3d)). Sid Meier's rule for early tuning is to **double a value or cut it in half** rather than nudge it by 5%, so every test gives a clear answer ([Soren Johnson](https://www.designer-notes.com/?p=119)). Late in tuning, small changes still matter: Halo 3's sniper rifle changed from **0.5 to 0.7 seconds** between shots, a "tiny tweak" that changed the game ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-0-2-seconds-to-balancing-i-halo-3-i-s-sniper-rifle)).

### B3. Teams judge success by watching players, and they throw failures away

Valve treats designs as hypotheses and playtests as experiments, judged by watching players directly; its slides say playtesting is "definitely not focus testing" ([Valve, GDC 2009](https://cdn.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf)). Bungie's bar for Halo was "maybe 30 seconds of fun that happened over and over" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2011-07-14-half-minute-halo-an-interview-with-jaime-griesemer.html)). Mark Cerny's method ends early development with a "publishable first playable" and cancels the project if it fails, because a project "will not miraculously get better during Production" ([Janczewski on Cerny](http://www.mateuszjanczewski.pl/game-development-tips/the-game-development-method-by-mark-cerny/)). Supercell, one of the few studios to publish its failures, killed **7 of the 10** games discussed in one account at the prototype stage ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-supercell-kills-its-darlings-to-focus-on-potential-hits/)). The near-universal rule for code is to **keep the knowledge and throw away the prototype code**: prototype code should be "agile and fast," not robust ([Juul's notes on Hecker & Gingold](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2006/04/04/my-gdc-2006-notes/)), and reusing it "looks like a time saver" but often backfires ([Macrobian Games](https://macrobiangames.com/blog/unity-prototype-vs-production-readiness/)). One caution covers this whole section: famous successes are the ones that get written up, so failed projects that used the same methods are mostly invisible.

### B4. Physics-character inventors made the same five moves

**Euphoria began as biology.** Torsten Reil, a zoology doctoral student at Oxford, used **genetic algorithms** (programs that breed the best of many random attempts, with small mutations, over many generations) to evolve **central pattern generators** (small neural networks that produce rhythmic signals, like those in an animal's spinal cord) for a simulated walker. Runs started with 100 random characters and were eventually cut to about **20 generations** and a few minutes of computing ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/36671)). The 2002 paper reported stable walking on flat ground **without the walker sensing its own joints** ([Reil & Husbands](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~philh/pubs/reilhusbandsIEEE.pdf)). NaturalMotion first sold the idea offline as Endorphin, "virtual stuntmen" for films such as *Troy*, where a failed take could simply be thrown away ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/43028)). Only then did it run live in games as Euphoria, described as AI controller modules driving simulated muscles on top of a rigid-body physics core ([AWN](https://www.awn.com/news/naturalmotion-announces-euphoria-next-gen-game-platforms)). Even in GTA IV its typical job was to take over for a few seconds during hits and falls and then hand back to **motion capture** (recorded movement of real actors), while walking and running stayed animation-driven ([AiGameDev](http://aigamedev.com/open/editorial/naturalmotion-euphoria/)). At every stage NaturalMotion chose a job that asked less of the controller than full-time control would: film takes that could be discarded, then a few seconds at a time inside games. NaturalMotion's own football game, Backbreaker, was marketed with "no two tackles are the same" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backbreaker_(video_game))), which is exactly the kind of variation this game wants.

**The indie cases tell the same story in smaller form.** Gang Beasts used physics characters "because we didn't have an animator" ([GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/funny-how-changing-face-comedy-modern-video-games)), and the physics "dictated even the game's iconic character design" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/?p=215072)). Human: Fall Flat's creator got "the character more or less right" before building any puzzles, and replaced separate sticks for each arm, which made "really messy controls," with one trigger per arm ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/human-fall-flat-interview-tomas-sakalauskas/)). Landfall's balance script for Totally Accurate Battle Simulator "adds force to the legs and feet," so that "if both feet are in front of the body's center of mass one of them needs to move backwards," and the studio later split "keep upright," ground checks and leg swing into separate pieces of code ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-landfall-games-finds-the-fun-in-physics-engines)). Toribash replaced its ball joints with **18 joints that each move on one axis and have four states** ([GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/reference/articles/article2341.asp)), QWOP gave the player four keys for thighs and calves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QWOP)), and Baby Steps began as "a couple of boxes for feet, glowing spheres for a butt, and lines for legs" ([Game Rant](https://gamerant.com/baby-steps-prototype-original-version-game-history-funny/)). Baby Steps' walking still took "many years of iteration," and lifting each foot by hand "felt better" than automatic lifting ([Game Rant](https://gamerant.com/baby-steps-interview-gamble-walking-gameplay-only/)). Exanima, where "all character motions are the result of physical forces and virtual muscles," calls its system "a huge beast in need of constant upgrades," and once retuned arm muscle tension because players found attacks weak and hard to control ([Steam news](https://store.steampowered.com/news/posts/?feed=steam_community_announcements&appids=362490&enddate=1558211311)).

**Animation inventors followed the same pattern.** Overgrowth's David Rosen built fluid movement from "very few key frames" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020049/Animation-Bootcamp-An-Indie-Approach)); one student reimplementation uses **four poses per walk or run cycle** and blends between them according to speed ([TAMU demo](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/sueda/courses/CSC474/2016W/demos/ismithgr/index.html)). Spore had to animate creatures that players would invent after release, so animators recorded intent ("this hand reaches here") and an **inverse-kinematics solver** (code that works out joint angles from where a hand or foot must go) fitted that intent to each body ([Hecker](https://chrishecker.com/Real-time_Motion_Retargeting_to_Highly_Varied_User-Created_Morphologies)). Ubisoft's motion matching began as a deliberately "brute-force" simplification of a complex research method ([Game Watch](https://game.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/news/749136.html); [arXiv review](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22632v1)).

**Researchers took the same route.** Raibert and Hodgins borrowed controllers from hopping robots, in which **foot placement is the main lever for balance** ([CMU](https://ri.cmu.edu/publications/animation-of-dynamic-legged-locomotion)), and Hodgins' team then made a simulated human run, cycle and vault ([CMU](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/animating-human-athletics)). **SIMBICON** (2007) reduced two-legged control to a few target poses plus **one simple rule** that places the stepping foot according to where the body's **centre of mass** (its overall balance point) is and how fast it is moving, using the midpoint of the hips as a cheap stand-in for the centre of mass ([SIMBICON paper](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2007-siggraph-simbicon.pdf)). Coros and colleagues (2010) combined standard robotics tools into a walker that needs no tuning for each new character, and released the code ([UBC](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc)). DeepMimic (2018) used **reinforcement learning** (a program that improves by trial, error and reward), and its breakthrough was two training tricks found by switching features off one at a time ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.02717)).

| Case | Starting constraint | First crude version | Key trick | Lesson for the mannequin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wright brothers | Nobody could control a glider | Kite, then a bicycle-wheel rig | Measure one job per rig | One Test Lab page per job |
| Euphoria | A biology question | Evolved walker on flat ground | Physics only in short windows at first | Earn reliability step by step |
| Human: Fall Flat | Solo developer, new controller | Physics arms, no puzzles | One trigger per arm | Few, dependable control channels |
| TABS (Landfall) | Many unit types, one body | Ragdoll plus a balance script | Push the feet under the centre of mass | A simple balance rule goes far |
| Toribash | Joints too chaotic to control | Ball joints | 18 one-axis, four-state joints | Fewer directions of movement first |
| Overgrowth | Tiny team | A few key poses | Springy, speed-driven blending | Few poses plus physics-like curves |
| SIMBICON | Two-legged bodies fall over | A sequence of target poses | One foot-placement rule | Cheap balance feedback |
| Spider-Man | The swing must feel great | Physics swing, built first | Years of hidden helpers | Hidden assists instead of meters |

### B5. Two families of design, and why a hybrid fits this mannequin

The cases split into two families. **Physics-first with helper forces** is the indie route. The body is a ragdoll, and invisible helper forces hold the torso up or push the feet under the body; Ubisoft's Simon Clavet calls these "god forces" ([GDC Vault, Ragdoll Motion Matching](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026712/contactUs)). This route is simple to start and full of surprises, but it leans toward clumsiness. Human: Fall Flat's studio cancelled its sequel because it went "too stiff. Too polished" and lost its clumsy charm ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/-human-fall-flat-2-is-cancelled-we-are-making-human-fall-flat-3-no-brakes-games-founder-looks-back-on-a-defining-decade)); that warns against over-tightening a comedy game, not against athletic movement.

**A plan that physics follows** is the big-studio route. An animation or planning layer decides what the body should do, and **joint motors** pull a physics body toward that pose, so collisions and momentum stay real. Naughty Dog's early attempts at this for Uncharted 4 were unpredictable before they found a way for animation to steer physics bodies predictably ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024087/Physics-Animation-in-Uncharted-4)). EA's "driven ragdolls" follow animation and react believably ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025210/Physics-Driven-Ragdolls-and-Animation)); Respawn compared motors, velocities and constraints and chose motors for Star Wars Jedi: Fallen Order ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/leveraging-physical-animation-to-sell-force-powers-in-i-jedi-fallen-order-i-)); Unreal Engine offers the same idea as a strength setting from 0 to 1 ([Epic](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Runtime/Engine/UPhysicalAnimationComponent)); and Ubisoft's DReCon drives the body only through its own joint "muscles" while keeping game-level responsiveness ([Ubisoft La Forge](https://montreal.ubisoft.com/en/drecon-data-driven-responsive-control-of-physics-based-characters/)).

For an athlete with deliberate weight placement, the second family fits better. Fall Guys' lead designer named the reason: "When you press jump, the character needs to jump. And if they fall over, you should kind of understand why it happened" ([GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/fall-guys-interview); exact page not confirmed). But the owner's other goals (heavy arms, ragdoll follow-through, no two throws alike) need physics to do the actual moving. **The recommendation is therefore a hybrid: a planning layer sets targets, motors with limited strength chase them, and physics creates the result.** Two counterweights deserve respect. A 2012 review of two decades of research found that commercial games "still resort to kinematics-based approaches" (pure animation with no physics) for characters the player controls, because physics control is hard ([Geijtenbeek & Pronost](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/v31i8pp2492-2515)). And pure animation plus springs is the cheapest and safest option on phones. Both are reasons to keep a kinematic **Plan B** alive, not reasons to start there.

## C. The building blocks exist, but Rapier's browser version has gaps

### C1. A motor is a spring inside a joint

Rapier's joint motors are **PD controllers** (proportional-derivative controllers): a spring that pulls the joint toward a target angle, set by **stiffness**, plus a brake that resists speed, set by **damping**, with a cap on how much force the "muscle" can deliver, called **max force** ([Rapier docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dimforge/rapier.rs/master/docs/user_guides/templates/joints.mdx)). Rapier works out motor forces inside its **solver** (the part of the engine that calculates all forces each step) rather than adding them beforehand, which should give them the property of "Stable PD" controllers, designed so that strong springs do not blow up at normal game step sizes ([Tan, Liu & Turk](https://www.cc.gatech.edu/%7Eturk/my_papers/stable_pd.pdf); [Rapier motor_model.rs](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/src/dynamics/joint/motor_model.rs)). That is an inference from how the code is built and should be confirmed in testing.

One detail matters a great deal for heavy arms. Rapier's default motor model scales its springs by mass so that "heavy and light objects respond similarly" ([motor_model.rs](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/src/dynamics/joint/motor_model.rs)). Left alone, **a heavier arm would not feel heavier**. Heaviness has to come from a capped max force, a softer spring, or the alternative force-based model, whose springs "produce absolute forces." The Jolt engine's guide gives a useful starting range for spring "frequency" at 60 physics steps a second: about **20 for a stiff spring and 2 for a soft one**, with a damping ratio of 1 meaning the joint arrives without overshooting ([Jolt Architecture](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics/blob/master/Docs/Architecture.md)).

### C2. Version 0.20 or newer is required, and ball joints still cannot be limited

This is the most time-sensitive finding. Rapier's JavaScript version had **no motors on ball (spherical) joints** from early 2022 until **version 0.20.0 on 8 August 2026**, which added them one axis at a time; the published 0.19.3 package has none, and 0.21.0 (25 September 2026) has them ([Rapier TypeScript changelog](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/bindings/typescript/CHANGELOG.md); [0.21.0 type definitions](https://unpkg.com/@dimforge/rapier3d-compat@0.21.0/dist/dynamics/impulse_joint.d.ts)). The motor targets are **three separate angles, not one combined rotation**, so the planning layer's rotations must be converted, and how Rapier measures those angles during large shoulder rotations is not documented. In the JavaScript version, **ball joints have no bend limits** (only hinge and slider joints do), and the alternative "multibody" joints have neither motors nor limits ([0.21.0 type definitions](https://unpkg.com/@dimforge/rapier3d-compat@0.21.0/dist/dynamics/multibody_joint.d.ts)). Rapier's Rust version has all of these, but using them would mean compiling a custom build. The workarounds are hinge joints at the elbows and knees, as Rapier's own ten-piece test ragdoll uses ([ragdolls3.rs](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/examples3d/stress_tests/ragdolls3.rs)), clamping motor targets inside a natural cone in code, or relying on capped force and damping for a soft end of range. A hinge elbow would change the brief's "ball joints everywhere" even if the visible ball stays, so that is the owner's decision. The project today contains Rapier only as an old 0.12 copy pulled in by three.js type files, and three.js's ready-made Rapier add-on loads version 0.17.3 with a varying step size, so that add-on should not be used for this character.

### C3. "Never stretch" needs measuring, because standard joints can drift

Rapier offers two joint systems. **Impulse joints**, the kind games usually use, "can be violated if the solver does not converge," which means two pieces can drift slightly apart under stress. **Multibody joints** "cannot be violated at all," but in the browser version they cannot be driven by motors ([Rapier docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dimforge/rapier.rs/master/docs/user_guides/templates/joint_constraints.mdx)). Because the rigid pieces themselves never bend, "stretch" can only appear as a gap opening at a joint, so every test page should display the **gap at each joint in millimetres**. Several settings reduce drift: more solver passes (the default is 4, and extra passes can be given to the arm pieces alone) ([0.21.0 integration parameters](https://unpkg.com/@dimforge/rapier3d-compat@0.21.0/dist/dynamics/integration_parameters.d.ts)); switching off collisions between joined neighbours, which also permits the overlaps the brief allows ([ragdolls3.rs](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/examples3d/stress_tests/ragdolls3.rs)); keeping neighbouring pieces' weights within a comfortable ratio (about 2 to 1 is fine, about 10 to 1 tends to jitter); and locking an axis rather than allowing a tiny range of a few degrees ([Unity manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/Manual/RagdollStability.html)).

One newer limit needs testing. Since the 2026 releases (JavaScript version 0.20 onward), Rapier caps rotation at about **45 degrees per physics step** ([Rapier changelog](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/CHANGELOG.md)). At 60 steps a second that is 2,700 degrees per second, while an elite pitcher's shoulder reaches about **7,500 degrees per second** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3392154)). Running physics at 120 steps a second, the step size `motion-core` already uses for movement, doubles the cap. The research found no way to switch the cap off in the browser version.

### C4. Phone performance is the largest unmeasured risk

No published measurement exists for Rapier, or any browser physics engine, running jointed bodies on a mid-range phone. The indirect signs are good. Rapier's own stress test piles up **125 ragdolls with 1,250 bodies**, although no timing is published ([ragdolls3.rs](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/examples3d/stress_tests/ragdolls3.rs)), and a desktop test ran 4,500 bodies at 120 frames a second in 2D ([DEV](https://dev.to/jerzakm/this-little-known-javascript-physics-library-blew-my-mind-57oo)); the mannequin has 17. The real risks lie elsewhere. The 0.21.0 package is about **1.65 MB compressed in its all-in-one form, roughly double version 0.19.3**, and 0.21.0 switched on soft-body features in the published builds ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@dimforge/rapier3d-compat); sizes measured from the package during the research). When physics falls behind, a "spiral of death" can drop frame rates to single digits ([Godot](https://godotengine.org/article/upcoming-serious-web-performance-boost/)). Drawing is less worrying: about 100 to 200 **draw calls** (separate drawing commands sent to the graphics chip) is a rough budget for low-end phones ([PlayCanvas](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/optimization/guidelines/)), and 17 pieces use a small share of that. Rapier has a built-in profiler for exactly this measurement ([TypeScript changelog](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier/blob/master/bindings/typescript/CHANGELOG.md)).

The precedents are mixed. Passive (unpowered) Rapier ragdolls run in three.js ([DEV](https://dev.to/mattvb91/realistic-ragdoll-physics-in-threejs-1pko)); the JoltPhysics.js browser engine has a working "Powered Rig" demo, and its shoulder-style joints have both limits and motors ([JoltPhysics.js](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics.js/blob/main/README.md); [Jolt Architecture](https://github.com/jrouwe/JoltPhysics/blob/master/Docs/Architecture.md)); and physics-based animation has shipped on phones in Gibbon: Beyond the Trees ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/deep-dive-physics-based-animation-in-gibbon-beyond-the-trees)). **No active ragdoll built on Rapier in a browser has been published.** Jolt is therefore the proven fallback if Rapier's gaps cause trouble.

### C5. Motor-control science gives a recipe for "no two throws identical"

The scientist Nikolai Bernstein called skilled movement "repetition without repetition": an expert gets the same result while the details vary from try to try ([Ranganathan et al.](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.02018/full)). In throwing, the hand's position and speed at the moment of release decide where the ball goes, while the rest of the movement can vary freely ([Wilson et al.](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/10760/1/AffordancesForThrowingAnUncontrolledManifoldAnalysisPV-WILSON.pdf)). Human muscle signals carry noise that **grows with effort** ([Harris & Wolpert](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/29528)), and the nervous system corrects only the errors that would spoil the goal ([Todorov](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/%7Etodorov/papers/TodorovNIPS03.pdf)). Ken Perlin's smooth "coherent noise" gives animated characters the texture of life without simulating everything ([Perlin](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/468392)), and later research warns that real variation is more than random jitter added on top ([Lau et al.](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring11/cos598A/pdfs/Lau09.pdf)).

Together these give a recipe. Vary stance width, lean, elbow carry, wind-up depth and timing within natural ranges; let harder throws vary more; use smooth noise for idle sway and breathing; and keep the release position and timing under correction. A **seeded random generator** (one whose "random" numbers can be replayed exactly from a starting number) keeps every throw reproducible for testing. Physics adds variation of its own, because slightly different starting states lead to different results, as Backbreaker's tackles showed. No source measures how much variation reads as "natural" rather than "drunk," so that judgment belongs to the owner's eye on a real phone.

## D. A twelve-step process for inventing the mannequin's movement

The steps below apply the core loop from A4 to this mannequin: 17 rigid pieces (chest, belly, pelvis, upper arms, forearms, hands, thighs, calves, feet, neck and head) joined by 16 joints, including the two hidden spine joints. They also follow the owner's working rules: research and compare before each step, explain the plan, and wait for "Go." Each step names what gets built, the question it answers, a pass test written before building, and what only the owner can judge. **The numeric targets are proposed starting points to agree on, not research findings.** Test pages live in the Test Lab as throwaway rigs; only proven pieces move into `motion-core`, during the clean rebuild the owner has already planned. The project notes record why this matters: the rigid ball-jointed avatar is central to the owner's aim of throws and shots that look unlike other games.

```
 Player input ──► PLANNING LAYER  (where should each piece be?)
                  balance, foot planning, reaching, key poses, variation
                          │  target angles for the 16 joints
                          ▼
                  JOINT MOTORS  (springs with a strength cap)
                          │  forces
                          ▼
                  RAPIER PHYSICS  (gravity, momentum, contacts)
                          │  positions of the 17 rigid pieces
                          ▼
                  three.js draws each piece exactly as simulated
```

### Step 1. Write the movement spec as jobs with tests

Turn the goals into a one-page list of jobs: stand and balance, shift weight, step, walk and run, turn, wind up and throw, follow through and recover, fall and get up. For each job, write one number test and one eye test before anything is built, for example "the centre of mass stays over the patch of ground between the feet while standing" and "the owner says the arm looks heavy." This pairing matches the project's avatar direction, which asks for motion judged by how it looks and feels as well as by numerical checks. Then name the hardest unsolved job, as the Wrights named control. The research suggests it is **keeping a motor-driven body balanced while its own heavy arms swing**, because every other goal depends on it.

### Step 2. Prove the engine works on the owner's phone

This is the bicycle-wheel test. Build a throwaway page where the 17 pieces and 16 joints fall as a passive ragdoll, once in Rapier 0.21 and, side by side, once in JoltPhysics.js. Show on screen the load time, physics milliseconds per step, frame rate and the largest joint gap. Try 60 and 120 physics steps a second, and 4 and 8 solver passes. A proposed pass: a steady 60 frames a second, physics under about 2 ms of the 16.7 ms each frame allows, and joint gaps small enough to hide inside the permitted overlaps. If neither engine passes, switch to the kinematic Plan B before investing further. Adding either library needs the owner's approval first.

### Step 3. Chart the options and pick two or three to try

Make a Zwicky box with one row per choice. How the body is driven: pure animation with springs, an arms-only ragdoll, a full body with helper forces, or a full body with joint motors only. How balance works: a helper force on the pelvis, foot placement from the centre of mass, or both. How joint limits are handled: hinge elbows and knees, or ball joints limited in code. Where variation comes from: target ranges, effort-scaled noise, smooth noise, physics itself. Cross out combinations that clash and keep two or three alive, as Toyota does.

### Step 4. Build the measuring tools before the movement

Give every rig the same instruments: sliders named in feel words ("arm heaviness," "time to reach the pose," "balance strength") instead of raw engine numbers; saved presets with an A/B switch to flip between two versions instantly; graphs of speed over time; slow motion; the joint-gap readout; and replay of any throw from its random seed. Use double-or-halve changes while exploring, and fine changes only once the right range is found.

### Step 5. The arm on a post

Fix the legs and pelvis in place and let one arm swing through a throw, driven by its motors. This tests heavy arms without solving balance first. Compare three versions in parallel: soft springs with a capped force, the force-based motor model, and pure animation with springs (Plan B). It passes when the owner, watching side by side on the phone, picks a version that looks heavy and still sends the hand where it was aimed.

### Step 6. Standing balance and weight shift

Free the legs. Start with a weak, capped helper force on the pelvis that acts only while a foot is planted, like training wheels. Add a SIMBICON-style rule that places the stepping foot according to the centre of mass's position and speed, and a Landfall-style trigger that takes a step when the centre of mass drifts outside the feet. Push the mannequin from several directions and record how much helper force it needed. The aim is to shrink that number release by release, correcting only what matters, as the minimal-intervention principle in C5 suggests.

### Step 7. Connect the planning layer to the motors

`motion-core` already holds kinematic layers for balance (centre of mass and support), reaching and pose blending, and its movement rules run in fixed 1/120-second steps; an earlier foot planner and walking cycle were removed in a reset but are kept in the project's git history. Feed these layers' output in as the target poses, converted into Rapier's three angles per joint. Test the conversion on its own, with numbers, across the full range of shoulder and hip movement, before trusting it in motion.

### Step 8. The throw on a balanced body

Build the throw from a few key poses (wind-up, stride, release, follow-through), timed as a chain from the hips outward to the hand; that order is standard throwing technique, but these research notes did not include a biomechanics source for it, so confirm the timing against one. After release, lower the arm's motor strength briefly so it swings through like a ragdoll, then restore it. With no meters or aiming guides, the body itself must show how hard the throw is, through wind-up depth and lean. Prototype two or three input styles in parallel, keep the control channels few and dependable, as Human: Fall Flat and Toribash learned, and use hidden assists where the player's intent and the physics disagree, as Spider-Man and Celeste do.

### Step 9. Add natural variation and prove it

Apply the recipe from C5. Run 20 seeded throws with identical input: the details that do not matter (stance, lean, timing) should differ every time, while the release stays within the intended range. Only the owner can judge whether the result reads as natural or drunk.

### Step 10. Falls and getting up

When balance is lost or the mannequin is hit, lower motor strength so physics takes over for a moment, as Euphoria did. Once it settles, detect whether it lies face up or face down and ramp strength back up toward a get-up sequence of poses, a strength-ramp version of Unreal's snapshot-and-blend method ([Epic](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/animation-pose-snapshot-in-unreal-engine)).

### Step 11. Combine one job at a time, and watch real players

Add each passing job to the full character and re-run every earlier rig test so nothing breaks unnoticed. Then watch people play on phones without coaching, as Valve does, and ask whether a 30-second loop of throwing stays fun.

### Step 12. Keep the knowledge, rebuild cleanly, keep tuning

Write down the winning values, formulas and lessons. Rebuild the system cleanly in `motion-core` with full tests, and compare the rebuild against saved presets and recorded seeded throws so the feel does not drift. Then expect years of tuning, and protect whatever looseness gives the mannequin its character.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the question. Inventing this movement system is less about finding one clever idea and more about **building the measuring setup that lets ordinary ideas be tested quickly**. The Wrights' decisive invention was arguably the wind tunnel, and this project's equivalent is a set of Test Lab rigs with feel-word sliders, graphs, joint-gap readouts and replayable throws. A second, less obvious takeaway is that the owner's most distinctive goals are the ones physics delivers most cheaply. Momentum, follow-through and "no two throws identical" come almost free from a motor-driven rigid body, as Backbreaker's tackles showed, while the expensive part, which took Baby Steps years and keeps Exanima in constant repair, is balance and trustworthy control. That is why balance under the arm's own swing is the job to crack first.

The newest risk is also the least documented: Rapier's browser motors are about three months old, ball-joint limits are still missing, and nobody has published an active Rapier ragdoll or measured one on a phone, so the first test is a small piece of original research in its own right. What happens next is the owner's decision: whether to approve a throwaway phone test of Rapier 0.21 with JoltPhysics.js beside it (Step 2), and whether hinge-style elbows and knees are acceptable inside the visible ball joints if the browser version cannot limit ball joints. Everything after that follows from what the phone test measures.
